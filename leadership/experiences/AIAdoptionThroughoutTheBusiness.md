### S — Situation

The organisation — roughly 1,000 people, with a department of 200–250 across engineering, QA, product, UX, design, and management — had invested over £100,000 in GitHub Copilot and was under board-level pressure to justify that investment through increased delivery speed.

A Community of Practice had been set up to drive AI adoption, but the proposed approach was to move the entire department from traditional SDLC practices directly to an AI-first model where all functions would merge into a single unit. No one had validated what AI could actually do within each function. No boundaries had been defined. The risk was that two hundred people would pull in different directions, burn trust in AI before it had proven itself, and the initiative would produce noise but no lasting change.

---

### T — Task

I volunteered to help steer the COP toward a more grounded approach. When the original lead went on long-term sick leave, I was asked to take over and drive the initiative fully. My mandate was clear: achieve 25% AI-written code as an initial milestone and increase productivity across all functions in the department.

---

### A — Actions

**1. Reframed the strategy from the start**

Rather than letting everyone dive in without direction, I mapped the SDLC and identified which areas AI could support, augment, or remove — then prioritised by value versus effort. The approach was deliberate: prove value within each function first, before attempting to merge functions or make broader claims about what AI could replace.

**2. Built a community and created momentum**

I spoke at a company town hall to generate visibility and interest, then recruited volunteers from across the department. This gave the initiative a coalition of advocates across functions rather than being driven top-down.

**3. Pushed back on the board-level narrative**

Senior leadership was focused almost entirely on delivery speed as the measure of ROI. I challenged this directly — pointing out that none of our OKRs mentioned speed, but all of them focused heavily on quality. I made the case that chasing speed without quality would accumulate tech debt, security gaps, and performance issues that would ultimately slow delivery more than any AI gain could recover. After several conversations, leadership agreed to reframe the core pillars as quality and delivery speed together.

**4. Handled enthusiastic but unworkable ideas carefully**

When a Security Director presented a ChatGPT-based testing solution, I didn't dismiss it. I arranged a meeting, asked him questions that guided him to identify the gaps himself — how it would get context, how it would produce consistent results on a complex enterprise system. I then shared my own parallel work on the same problem, and together we identified where each approach fell short and agreed to continue refining it collaboratively with the test team. He became an advocate rather than a detractor.

**5. Coached across all functions**

I worked with engineers, testers, product owners, UX designers, and managers individually — understanding each role's day-to-day complexity and identifying where AI could genuinely streamline it. I created agents and coached others on how to build and think about them. Where there was resistance, I left that area and returned to it later once trust had been established elsewhere.

---

### R — Result

**Delivery metrics (2026 vs 2025):**

- 25% AI-written code target achieved
- 41% more work items completed on average
- 15% higher velocity
- 20% more story points delivered
- 10% reduction in PBI cycle time

**People and function impact:**

Product owners are now producing more complete PBIs that meet the definition of ready, faster. UX are using AI to build out specs and prototypes that communicate their intent more clearly, which has reduced friction as work passes to developers and helped break down silos between functions. Engineers are using agentic workflows to deliver work and write tests faster. Managers have been able to automate the reporting and administrative work they found least rewarding, freeing them to focus more time on their people — something several managers specifically called out as a meaningful shift in how they spend their day.

The initiative is ongoing. New problems surface regularly and I continue working across all functions to find better ways of working and overcome new obstacles as they arise.

---

### L — Learning

Action breeds clarity — but action without a plan to expand creates silos. The most important thing I took from this was the value of diversity of thinking: the people who push back and find the flaws in an idea are as valuable as the people who build. Getting both in the room, working toward a common goal, produces something far stronger than either group would achieve alone.

I also learned how critical upward reporting is during change. Keeping leadership clearly informed on progress — not just outcomes, but the pace and state of change — is what maintains their confidence and buys you the space to keep going.
