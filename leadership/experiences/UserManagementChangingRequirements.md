### **S — Situation**

My team took over an epic that had already been partially worked on by another team.

The scope had originally been agreed, with additional work planned only if time allowed. However, when my team picked it up, we began receiving **new requirements** that were assumed to have been part of the original scope.

At the same time:

- Additional **UX/styling feedback** was being raised during development
- These changes were **undocumented** and often **time-consuming**
- This created a risk to **delivery timelines and sprint commitments**

---

### **T — Task**

My responsibility was to:

- Clarify and stabilise the scope
- Align Product and Design with engineering realities
- Protect the team from ongoing disruption
- Ensure we could still deliver value predictably

---

### **A — Actions**

### 1️⃣ Validate and challenge assumptions

- I leveraged my knowledge of the previous team to confirm these were **new requirements**, not missed ones
- Spoke with my team and agreed we would **accept changes initially**, but clearly highlight the delivery impact

---

### 2️⃣ Surface the problem early

- As changes continued, I identified a pattern:
    - Requirements were **implicit rather than defined**
    - Design expectations were **not documented upfront**

---

### 3️⃣ Facilitate a stakeholder alignment conversation

I brought together:

- Product Owner
- UX Designer

In that discussion:

- They believed they weren’t changing requirements, just clarifying expectations
- I reframed this by showing the **impact on delivery**, including:
    - Increased scope
    - Rework
    - Reduced predictability

---

### 4️⃣ Introduce clear working agreements

To resolve this, I established a structured approach:

### 🔹 Define what “done” means

- All requirements must be **fully documented in the story**
- Any missing detail is treated as **new scope**

### 🔹 Separate standard vs custom work

- If using **default UI components**, this was explicitly stated
- Any deviation required **UX designs upfront**

### 🔹 Control mid-sprint changes

- Changes during a sprint were only allowed if:
    - They didn’t impact delivery
    - Or were necessary to avoid broken functionality

---

### 5️⃣ Balance flexibility with delivery

- I made it clear we were **not blocking change**, but:
    - It needed to be **visible, agreed, and prioritised**
- This helped shift the mindset from:
    - “Add as we go” → “Plan and commit intentionally”

---

### **Conflict Element**

There was a clear **misalignment in perspective**:

- Product/UX: “These are expected features”
- Engineering: “These are new requirements impacting delivery”

I handled this by:

- Using **specific examples and delivery impact**
- Keeping the conversation **objective and outcome-focused**
- Avoiding blame, and instead focusing on **process improvement**

---

### **R — Result**

- Scope became **clearly defined and agreed upfront**
- Reduced mid-sprint disruption
- Improved **predictability of delivery**
- Stronger alignment between Engineering, Product, and UX

The team was able to focus on delivering committed work without constant rework or ambiguity.

---

### **L — Learning**

- Misalignment often comes from **implicit expectations, not bad intent**
- Clear definitions of scope and “done” are critical for delivery
- EMs play a key role in **translating between technical and non-technical perspectives**
- Balancing flexibility and structure is essential to maintain **both agility and predictability**
